Test Scheduling Service

TSS — fleet architecture

A job broker for physical hardware. A testbed agent is a machine; the resources it owns are the devices cabled to it. Agents register themselves and their inventory; jobs declare which devices they need; the scheduler claims all of them or none, and hands the owning machine a time-limited lease. Every failure reduces to one question — is this machine alive, and who owns these devices?

1 · Components and data flow

Agents always dial out. TSS never opens a connection into a testbed — that is what makes the design work across offices behind NAT, and it is the decision everything else rests on.

That single choice is why this works through office NAT, why an agent only takes work when its hardware is genuinely ready, and why scaling to offices in three countries needs no firewall rules at all.

2 · The unit of allocation — a bench is not one thing

Liveness is a property of the machine; allocation is a property of the device. Two jobs share one bench freely as long as they need disjoint devices — but a job never holds part of what it needs. That rule is the deadlock prevention. Keeping a job on one bench is not — see the note below.

Co-location is not what prevents deadlock — all-or-nothing is. Every device is a row in one table in one database, so a claim spanning five benches is the same single local transaction as a claim on one, and equally safe. Cross-bench allocation would work. A job is kept to one bench because the devices are physically cabled to each other, and because a job spanning k benches dies if any of the k dies. Say that precisely: “we avoid deadlock by keeping jobs on one bench” is wrong, and wrong in a way that shows you don't know which mechanism is doing the work.

Do not accept an “acquire in a loop, release on failure” implementation. It looks equivalent and isn't: if the process dies between acquiring device 2 and failing on device 3, those two devices are locked with no owner and no lease to expire them. A transaction rolls back on crash; hand-rolled cleanup does not.

The failure mode of all-or-nothing is starvation, not deadlock — and the fix is reservation, which is not a partial hold. A job needing three devices with one free takes nothing. The scheduler simply withholds that free device from other jobs: it stays free in the database with no owner, the job stays QUEUED, and the mark lives in scheduler memory, recomputed every pass. Deadlock needs hold-and-wait; this is wait-without-hold, so no cycle can form. Reservations cover exactly one feasible bench — a machine with two healthy VGs can never satisfy a three-VG job, so reserving there would idle a device forever. And if no bench is feasible at all, the job is flagged no_capable_agent rather than waiting silently, because a scheduler stuck on an impossible job is indistinguishable from a broken one.

3 · Chaos: what happens when a bench dies mid-job

The lease expires on its own — nobody has to detect anything. Every job on that bench comes back, once each. And when the dead agent returns with a result for a job someone else now owns, the epoch fences it out.

Two things on this slide win the interview. The first is the epoch: without it, agent-7's stale “PASSED” overwrites a run that is still in flight, and a firmware engineer ships on a result from an abandoned job. The second is the word DISTINCT — a bench running one job across three of its devices appears three times if you iterate resources. Requeue per-device and you bump the epoch three times and burn the job's whole retry budget on a single bench failure. Nothing errors; the job just quietly dead-letters early.

4 · State machines

Splitting machine from device gives three small machines instead of one overloaded one. Two transitions carry the customer story: QUARANTINED stops one broken bench from looking like a firmware regression, and INFRA_ERROR stops our hardware failures from being reported as the engineer's bug.

FAILED vs INFRA_ERROR is a one-line schema decision that determines whether anyone trusts the system. Conflate them and engineers learn “the rig is flaky, just re-run it” — and then real regressions get re-run away. Separate them and a failed job means something.
